Methodological introduction

From sentence to analysis

Sentence Sense Detective is built around a simple learning task: look closely at a highlighted word, phrase, or clause; decide what it is or what function it has; then compare your analysis with a concise explanation. This chapter explains how authentic corpus examples become classroom-ready grammar questions.

The fuller grammatical account of word classes, sentence elements, and clauses will be expanded by Martin Grad.

Learning goals

After working through this chapter, you should be able to:

  • distinguish word class, sentence element, and clause analysis;
  • explain why a machine-produced analysis cannot always be converted by simply renaming one label;
  • distinguish straightforward correspondences from cases that require structural interpretation or expert checking;
  • understand why a highlighted target may be a whole phrase, a clause, or a discontinuous unit rather than a single word;
  • use feedback as a grammatical explanation rather than as a bare statement of the correct answer;
  • understand how a large question bank can grow while preserving scholarly and pedagogical quality.

What you should already know

You should be familiar with the basic difference between a word and a phrase, a phrase and a clause, and the form of an expression and its function in a sentence. You do not need to know how the source analysis is represented internally.

1. One sentence can be described at several levels

Consider the sentence:

Sarah and her daughters arrived first.

The highlighted sequence can be described in more than one compatible way:

  • Sarah is a proper noun;
  • daughters is a noun;
  • Sarah and her daughters is a coordinated noun phrase;
  • the whole phrase functions as the Subject (S) of the clause.

These statements answer different questions. The practice tool therefore separates three analytical levels.

LevelCentral questionTypical answers
Parts of speechWhat kind of word is highlighted?noun, pronoun, adjective, lexical verb, auxiliary, preposition
Sentence elementsWhat function does the highlighted word, phrase, or clause have?S, P, Operator, DO, IO, SC, OC, A
ClausesWhat kind of clause is highlighted, how is it formed, and what function does it have?nominal clause, adverbial clause, relative clause, finite clause, infinitival clause, PostM

Key idea

Grammatical form and grammatical function are related, but they are not the same thing. A word-class label cannot by itself determine a sentence-element function, and the internal structure of a clause does not by itself determine the function of that clause in the larger sentence.

2. Why interpretation is needed

A machine can process thousands of sentences consistently and identify words, lemmas, grammatical features, heads, dependants, and clause-like structures. Its representation is designed for computational consistency, however, not for any one classroom tradition. Four differences are especially important.

2.1 A head word is not always the full learning target

The old woman living in the basement needs help.

One word acts as the structural centre of the Subject phrase, but the useful target is the whole phrase, including its determiner, adjective, and postmodifier. A reliable exercise must recover the complete unit rather than highlight only its head.

2.2 Similar source structures can have different classroom functions

A complement, modifier, or prepositional phrase cannot always receive one fixed sentence-element label. The answer may depend on the governing verb, the clause pattern, whether the unit is obligatory, its meaning, and where it attaches.

That is why a shortcut such as “every prepositional phrase is an Adverbial” would create many neat-looking but incorrect questions.

2.3 A classroom category may combine several pieces of evidence

Paul is a good student.

For teaching purposes, is is analysed as P, while a good student is SC. The result follows from the whole copular configuration rather than from one isolated label.

2.4 Some grammatical units are discontinuous

Did the children play?

The auxiliary occupies the Operator position, while the lexical verb supplies the predicator nucleus. A robust resource must support single words, continuous phrases and clauses, more than one highlighted unit, and—where the teaching task requires it—discontinuous units.

3. Three analytical paths

1

Straightforward correspondence

She meant no harm.

The highlighted noun phrase is a clear Direct Object (DO). Its extent is continuous and no competing analysis is plausible in the sentence.

2

Structural interpretation

The milk seems off.

The analysis must recognize a linking verb and a predicative complement. Off functions as SC here; its answer follows from the clause pattern, not from surface form alone.

3

Expert check

It is important to know the truth.

The sentence contains a formal subject and a postponed infinitival subject. Treating it as an ordinary lexical Subject would hide the construction.

Quality principle

Uncertainty is not repaired by forcing an answer. A doubtful example should be held back for expert checking rather than published with false confidence.

4. Worked examples

Example 1 · Recovering the whole Subject phrase

Sarah and her daughters arrived first.

Question: What is the function of the highlighted phrase?

Answer: S — Subject

The target is the complete coordinated phrase, not only one of its nouns.

Example 2 · Separating Operator and Predicator

Did the children play?

Analysis: Did is Operator; play is P — Predicator.

The inversion makes the distinction visible: the auxiliary carries the operator function, while the lexical verb provides the predicator nucleus.

Example 3 · Reconstructing a copular analysis

Paul is a good student.

Analysis: is is P; a good student is SC.

The complement identifies the Subject. The useful classroom analysis therefore separates the copular verb from the Subject Complement.

Example 4 · A clause functioning as Subject

To have seen him lose his temper was unexpected.

Answer: S — Subject

The target is a perfect infinitival clause, and the whole clause fills the Subject position in the larger clause.

Example 5 · A clause functioning as Adverbial

She telephoned while you were out.

Answer: A — Adverbial

The clause adds a temporal circumstance. Its type, structure, marker, and function are separate dimensions of analysis.

Example 6 · A relative clause functioning as Postmodifier

Students who work hard pass their exams.

Answer: PostM — Postmodifier

The clause narrows the reference of students. A fuller analysis also records its relative-clause type and restrictive interpretation.

5. How a corpus sentence becomes a question

  1. Sentence selection. Openly usable corpus material is filtered to remove fragments, broken boundaries, duplicates, private information, and unsuitable content.
  2. Source analysis. Each sentence receives a reproducible machine description that helps locate words, phrases, verbal structures, and clause-like units.
  3. Pedagogical interpretation. A separate grammatical profile states what evidence supports a classroom category, what blocks it, how the full target is recovered, and whether the result should be published or checked.
  4. Question construction. An accepted analysis is given a clear prompt, a highlighted target, three alternatives from the same category set, and a concise explanation.
  5. Quality control. The project checks target extent, answer terminology, the plausibility of alternatives, duplication, and the accuracy of the explanation.
  6. Classroom correction. Teacher feedback, student reports, and expert revision become recorded changes to the data and grammatical profile.

The presentation does not decide the grammar

The web page displays an already justified analysis. Scoring, answer order, and visual design must never determine the grammatical label.

6. What students are expected to learn

Students are expected to learn how to:

  • identify a word class in context;
  • recognize the complete unit performing a sentence function;
  • distinguish Subjects, Predicators, Operators, Objects, Complements, and Adverbials;
  • distinguish clause type, structure, marker, and function;
  • justify an analysis from the sentence rather than from a memorized surface cue.

Students are not expected to:

  • memorize the internal labels used during corpus preparation;
  • treat every phrase with the same form as having the same function;
  • accept a score without reading the explanation;
  • assume that one analytical tradition exhausts every possible grammatical description.

7. Why the reviewed core matters

The English pilot began with 106 examples reviewed by Martin Grad. They establish the teaching terminology, specify the correct highlighted units, expose cases where simple correspondence is insufficient, and identify constructions that should be withheld for expert checking.

A large bank is valuable only if expansion does not silently change the grammar. The reviewed core therefore acts as a stable reference: later versions must reproduce its accepted analyses or document why a correction has been made.

8. How the project handles uncertainty

  1. Do not confuse frequency with certainty. A common pattern may still be structurally ambiguous.
  2. Do not generalize from one attractive example. A generalization must survive contrasting cases.
  3. Do not use a score as a substitute for grammatical justification. A high numerical value does not make an analysis theoretically correct.
  4. Prefer omission to misinformation. A smaller accurate bank is more useful than a larger bank filled with doubtful examples.

Special care is required for formal and postponed Subjects, object-complement constructions, prepositional phrase attachment, linking verbs and copular clauses, reduced and non-finite clauses, zero relative clauses, restrictive and non-restrictive relatives, comparative clauses, and clauses complementing adjectives.

9. From English to a multilingual resource

The longer-term aim is not to impose English categories mechanically on every language. The reusable part is the architecture:

  1. a source analysis for the language;
  2. a language-specific grammatical profile;
  3. an approved teaching vocabulary;
  4. reviewed examples and safeguards;
  5. a common practice interface.

Each language taught at the Department of Translation Studies and across the Faculty of Arts can preserve its own grammatical tradition while using the same open technical infrastructure.

Transfer principle

The platform is shared; the grammar is not assumed to be identical.

10. How to use the practice tool effectively

Before answering

  1. What is the highlighted unit made of?
  2. What job does it perform in this sentence?

After an incorrect answer

Do not focus only on the correct label. Compare it with the full highlighted unit. Many mistakes arise because the learner identifies the right head word but the wrong complete phrase or clause.

During review rounds

Group mistakes by type: word class, form versus function, clause type versus function, target extent, or complement versus Adverbial. This makes the score diagnostically useful rather than merely competitive.

11. Check your understanding

  1. Why is the head word of a phrase not always an adequate highlighted target?
  2. In Did the children play?, why should Did and play not receive the same function label?
  3. Why can a prepositional phrase not always be assigned the same sentence-element function?
  4. What evidence may be needed to distinguish a Subject Complement from an Adverbial?
  5. Why should a doubtful automatically prepared example be withheld rather than published with a high confidence score?
  6. What part of the project can be shared across languages, and what must remain language-specific?
Suggested answers
  1. The pedagogical unit is often the full phrase, including modifiers and complements, while the source analysis may identify only its head.
  2. Did occupies the Operator position, while play is the lexical Predicator.
  3. Its function depends on attachment, the governing verb, obligatoriness, meaning, and the wider clause structure.
  4. The governing verb, the relationship between the phrase and the Subject, and the role of the phrase in completing the clause pattern.
  5. A numerical score describes system behaviour, not grammatical truth; uncertain constructions require expert judgement.
  6. The corpus-processing and practice architecture can be shared, while terminology, constructions, reviewed examples, and explanations must be developed for each language.

12. Chapter summary

  • Sentence Sense Detective separates word class, sentence element, and clause analysis.
  • Machine analysis supplies evidence, but classroom grammar requires a separate interpretive layer.
  • Some correspondences are straightforward; others depend on structural patterns; some must be checked by an expert.
  • Correct target extent is as important as the correct label.
  • The presentation layer displays justified analyses; it does not make grammatical decisions.
  • The 106 reviewed examples provide a stable reference core for expansion.
  • A multilingual platform can share infrastructure while preserving language-specific grammatical traditions.
